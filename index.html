How Hackathon Judges Think

Understanding what goes on inside a judge's head is the single biggest unfair advantage you can have.

Key Insight: Judges don't pick the best technology. They pick the team that makes them feel something — excitement, relief, or "why didn't I think of that?"

Every hackathon judge evaluates projects through a mental framework. If you understand that framework, you can engineer your project — and your pitch — to score maximum points in every category. Here are the eight pillars they evaluate.

Innovation

Judges see hundreds of "AI chatbots" and "to-do apps with GPT." Innovation isn't about using the newest model — it's about a novel application of technology to a problem nobody else noticed. Ask yourself: "Has this been done at Devpost 50 times?" If yes, pivot.

Execution

A working demo beats a beautiful slide deck every single time. Judges want to touch your product. Even if it's ugly, if it works end-to-end, you're ahead of 80% of teams. Prioritize the critical path: input → processing → output.

Presentation

Your pitch is a story, not a feature list. Structure: Problem → "What if?" → Demo → Impact. The first 15 seconds determine whether the judge pays attention or checks their phone.

Market Potential

Judges ask: "Could this become a real company?" Show TAM, SAM, SOM even with rough numbers. Mention one real competitor and explain why you're different. A $50B market with no solution is more exciting than a $1T market with 100 competitors.

Business Model

You don't need a full financial model. You need one clear sentence about how money flows. "We charge $29/month per clinic" is infinitely better than "We'll figure out monetization later."

Demo Quality

The demo is your product. Not your code, not your architecture — the demo. Pre-record a backup video. Have a fake user journey ready. Never type live if you can avoid it. One bug during a demo can kill your chances.

Real Problem

"We solved a problem" vs. "We found a problem and built a solution" — judges can tell the difference. A real problem has victims: people losing money, time, health, or opportunities. Name them.

Pro Tip: Before you build anything, write your judge pitch in one sentence. If it sounds boring, your project is boring. Fix the idea first.

Winning vs. Losing Examples

Real patterns from 200+ hackathons analyzed.

Winning Pattern

Teams that win focus on one measurable metric, demo a working slice end-to-end, and tell a story the judge remembers the next day. They fail fast, build the demo first, and only then add polish.

Losing Pattern

Teams that chase 10 features, finish none, and pitch "we could also..." on a broken live demo. They spend 20 hours building infrastructure nobody sees and 0 hours on the story.

The 10x Rule: A judge spends roughly 3–5 minutes per demo. In that time you must show the problem, the fix, and the proof. Every extra second on your tech stack is a second stolen from your impact story.

How To Find Million Dollar Problems

Great projects are built on painful problems. Here's a repeatable framework to find them in under an hour.

Rule of thumb: If you can't name the person who is hurting right now, you don't have a problem — you have a feature idea.

The 5-Step Problem Hunt
  1. Scratch your own itch
    List every frustration from the last 2 weeks: apps that annoyed you, manual tasks, wasted time. Your pain is a proxy for thousands of people.
  2. Mine complaint channels
    Search Reddit ("why is X so hard"), Twitter, app-store 1-star reviews, and Google Play reviews. Real users describe problems in their own words — use their vocabulary in your pitch.
  3. Study failed startups
    Read post-mortems on Failory and WhyRise. A startup that failed because of execution — not because the problem didn't exist — is a validation goldmine.
  4. Interview 5 real users
    Talk to anyone in the industry you're targeting. Ask "walk me through the last time this happened." Stories beat surveys every time.
  5. Quantify the pain
    Attach numbers: hours wasted per week, money lost per month, error rate. "Saves 6 hours a week" is a pitch. "Helps productivity" is noise.

The Pain Checker — score every idea

Give your idea a score out of 20. Below 12, keep hunting.

One-Sentence Problem Formula: "[User] wastes [amount] of [resource] every [timeframe] because [root cause]. Example: "Small retailers lose 8 hours every week because they reconcile GST invoices by hand."

Idea Red Flags
  • Requires 6+ months of work to demo anything
  • Depends on data you don't have access to
  • Needs hardware you can't buy at the hackathon
  • Already won 3 hackathons this year (check Devpost)
  • Solvable with an Excel sheet or a Google form

Reminder: You don't need a million-dollar problem to win. You need a painful problem you can demo in 24 hours. The idea vault in the next chapter is built exactly on that principle.

Winning Presentation Guide

The demo gets you noticed. The pitch gets you the trophy. Here's the exact structure winners use.

Rule #1: Never read from slides. Slides are your visual backup, not your script. You are the story.

The 7-Frame Pitch Structure
  1. The Hook (0–15s)
    One sentence that names the victim and the pain. "Pharmacists check 40 hand-written prescriptions a day by eye — and miss 20% of errors." No intro, no logos.
  2. The Problem (15–40s)
    Give the pain numbers: how many people, how much money, how often. One competitor, and why it's still unsolved.
  3. The "What if" (40–50s)
    The vision in one bold sentence. "What if an AI read every prescription in 2 seconds and caught the error before the patient did?"
  4. The Demo (50–120s)
    Show, don't tell. Narrate what's happening on screen. One flow, end-to-end. This is 60% of your time budget.
  5. The Magic Moment (120–150s)
    The single thing nobody expects — the live fall detection, the voice in Hindi, the price dropping live. One line: "Notice that."
  6. Business Model (150–175s)
    One sentence. "We charge hospitals ₹49 per 100 checks." Add TAM/SAM/SOM in a single line.
  7. The Close (175–180s)
    Restate the hook with the fix, end with a call to action ("We're looking for one pilot pharmacy"). Stop talking. Smile.

Complete Hackathon Checklist

Everything to do before, during, and after — so nothing leaks points.

Before the Hackathon
  • Register early — some events have waiting lists
  • Read the rules: judging criteria, allowed APIs, solo vs team
  • Form your team: 3–4 with clear roles (dev, dev, design, pitch)
  • Pick 3 candidate ideas from the vault and pre-validate them
  • Create all API accounts and generate keys in advance
  • Set up GitHub repo, branch protection off (speed matters)
  • Boilerplate ready: Next.js + Tailwind + Supabase skeleton
  • Charger, laptop, cables, hotspot, extension cord
  • Mock data and seed scripts prepared
  • Sleep — yes, before, not after
Day Zero (Opening Ceremony)
  • Attend sponsor talks — note the prizes and themes
  • Write down every sponsor's favorite API/tech
  • Decide the idea within 1 hour of the event start
  • Write your one-sentence pitch on a sticky note
  • Define the demo metric (what "winning" looks like)
During the Build
  • Build the vertical slice first: input → AI → output
  • Commit after every working milestone
  • One person owns the demo path; nobody breaks it
  • Pre-record the demo video by hour 20, not hour 23
  • Take breaks — a 20-minute walk fixes more bugs than staring
  • No gold-plating: features only if they help the demo

Pitch & Submission
  • Submit on Devfolio/Devpost 2 hours before deadline
  • Project title with a hook, not a name
  • Demo video under 3 minutes, with captions
  • Screenshots that tell the story even without video
  • Architecture diagram (one clean image)
  • Tech stack list + what each part does
  • Public repo link (README with demo link at top)
  • Team names + roles in the submission
  • Practice the 3-minute pitch 3 times before judging
  • Backup video uploaded to a cloud link
After the Hackathon
  • Thank the organizers and sponsors
  • Write a post-mortem: what worked, what didn't
  • Share the project on LinkedIn/Twitter with the demo link
  • Collect the sponsor contacts you met
  • Send follow-up emails within 48 hours
  • Decide: keep building, or keep the learnings and move on

The final secret: Winners don't have perfect projects. They have a clear story, a working demo, and the confidence to tell a judge "this is the one metric we moved." That's the entire game.
